01

How this case works

This is an interviewer-led case. Instead of you driving, the interviewer asks a series of questions, and each one tests a different skill: structure, break-even math, reading an exhibit, and pricing judgment. Answer each one crisply, then tie it back to the client's question.

NYU Stern's casebook rates each case for Quant and Structure from 1 to 10. This is an original case written in that style, rated 8 for quant and 6 for structure. The company and numbers are invented.

The math you'll use

Break-even, two ways

  • Contribution per session = (price − energy cost) × energy per session.
  • Break-even sessions = yearly fixed costs ÷ contribution per session.
  • Payback = build cost ÷ yearly profit.

Practice tools

  • A station calculator and a membership calculator.
  • Pilot-site data to test which locations pay back.

Ten terms to drop naturally

Kilowatt-hour (kWh)

The unit of energy chargers sell. A typical fast-charging session here delivers about 40 kWh.

Session

One car, one charge. Revenue is sessions times energy per session times price.

Utilization

How much of the day a charger is actually in use. Low utilization is the main reason charging stations lose money.

Contribution

What each session leaves after its own cost, the electricity, to help cover fixed costs.

Fixed costs

Costs that don't change with sessions: site lease, maintenance, software, and the utility's monthly demand charges.

Demand charge

A monthly utility fee based on a site's peak power draw. It's a big fixed cost for fast chargers.

Payback period

How many years of profit it takes to earn back the cost of building a station.

Membership pricing

A monthly fee in exchange for a lower price per unit. It rewards frequent customers.

Cannibalization

When a discount goes to customers who would have paid full price anyway.

Self-selection

Designing an offer so only the customers you want choose it, like a fee that only pays off for frequent users.

The case prompt

Interviewer

Our client, Quillfield Charging, runs fast electric-vehicle chargers at highway rest stops in the Northeast. It's building 40 new stations next year.

Today it charges a flat 45 cents per kilowatt-hour. Management is considering adding a 15 dollar monthly membership that cuts the price to 35 cents. The CEO wants to know: how should Quillfield price its charging, and where should it build?

Your move

Restate the question in one sentence before you do anything else.

Strong answer

"So Quillfield needs two decisions for its 40 new stations: whether to add a membership on top of the flat 45 cent price, and which sites to build at so each station earns its money back. Is that right?"

Interviewer: "Yes." Splitting the prompt into two decisions, pricing and location, sets up the whole case.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • What does success look like for the CEO?
    Each new station should be profitable and earn back its build cost within about 7 years.
  • Who are the customers?
    Two groups: commuters who charge on the same route most weekdays, about 30 percent of sessions, and long-distance travelers, about 70 percent.
  • What do competitors charge?
    Two other networks on the same highways charge 45 to 50 cents per kWh. Neither has a membership.
  • What does electricity cost Quillfield?
    About 15 cents per kWh, plus fixed yearly costs per station.

The first answer gives you the test for every station: profitable, and paid back in about 7 years. The customer split will matter for the membership question.

Question 1: what would you look at?

Interviewer

What factors would you consider to decide how Quillfield should price, and where it should build?

Your move

Take 60 seconds. Cover customers, station economics, pricing options, and competition.

Strong framework

How should Quillfield price, and where should it build?
1Customers
  • Commuters vs. travelers
  • How often each charges
  • What each will pay
2Station economics
  • Contribution per session
  • Fixed costs and break-even
  • Build cost and payback
3Pricing options
  • Flat price vs. membership
  • Time-of-day pricing
  • Who'd take a discount anyway
4Competition and sites
  • Rival prices and locations
  • Traffic at each site
  • Brand and reliability

Say it like this

"I'd look at four things. First, customers: commuters and travelers charge very differently and value different things. Second, station economics: what each session contributes, what a station costs to run, and how many sessions it needs to break even and pay back. Third, pricing options: whether a membership brings in more charging or just discounts people who'd pay full price anyway. Fourth, competition and sites: rival prices and how much traffic each location gets. I'd start with station economics, because that tells us what any price has to achieve."

Why it works

  • It covers both decisions in the prompt, pricing and location.
  • It flags cannibalization up front, the key risk of any discount.
  • It picks a starting point, which an interviewer-led case will then confirm or redirect.

Question 2: what does a station need to break even?

Interviewer

Price per kWh

$0.15
Energy per session
40 kWh

$108K / yr
Build cost per station
$540K
Chargers per station
4

Assume 360 days a year to keep the math clean. A session takes about 30 minutes.

Your move

Find the contribution per session first. Then divide fixed costs by it.

Hint: per kWh, then per session

Each kWh earns 45 cents and costs 15, so it leaves 30 cents. A session is 40 kWh. Multiply to get what one session contributes.

  1. 1 · Margin per kWh$0.45 − $0.15$0.30
  2. 2 · Per session$0.30 × 40 kWh$12
  3. 3 · Break-even$108,000 ÷ $12 = 9,000 a year ÷ 36025 a day
  4. 4 · Sanity check≈6 per charger × 30 min ≈ 3 hours, 13% of the day✓
  5. 5 · Profit at 45 a day(45 − 25) × $12 × 360$86.4K
  6. 6 · Payback$540,000 ÷ $86,4006.25 yrs

Quick check: if the price stays at 45 cents but electricity rises to 20 cents, what's the new break-even?

The margin drops to 25 cents, so a session contributes 10 dollars. 108 thousand divided by 10 is 10,800 a year, or 30 a day. A 5 cent cost increase raises break-even by 20 percent.

Question 3: which sites pay back?

Interviewer

Quillfield ran 8 pilot stations last year. Here are their average sessions a day. What does this tell you about where to build the 40 new ones?

Your move

Break-even tells you which sites make money. But the CEO's test is a 7-year payback. Work out the sessions a day that takes, then sort the sites.

  1. 1 · Profit for 7-year payback$540,000 ÷ 7≈$77K
  2. 2 · Extra sessions needed$77K ÷ $12 ÷ 360≈18 a day
  3. 3 · Payback threshold25 + 18≈43 a day
  4. 4 · Sort the pilots2 lose money · 3 too slow · 3 meet the target3 of 8
  5. 5 · The average trapAverage 36.5 a day → payback ≈ 11 years✗
  6. 6 · So whatBuild only where forecasts show 43+ a day✓

A pilot site averages 38 sessions a day. Should Quillfield build more sites like it?

13 sessions above break-even times 12 dollars times 360 days is about 56 thousand a year. 540 thousand divided by 56 thousand is about 9.6 years. Profitable is not the same as meeting the target.

Question 4: does the membership pay?

Interviewer

Now the membership: 15 dollars a month, and members pay 35 cents instead of 45. We surveyed commuters. Here's what they told us. Is the membership a good idea?

Your move

Compare what one commuter is worth per month today with what they're worth as a member. Then ask whether travelers would join.

Hint: two scenarios

A member's session earns 35 minus 15 cents, or 20 cents per kWh, times 40 kWh. Do the math twice: once if they keep charging 12 times, and once if they really move to 18.

  1. 1 · Commuter today12 sessions × $12$144 / mo
  2. 2 · Member's session($0.35 − $0.15) × 40 kWh$8
  3. 3 · Member, same 12 sessions12 × $8 + $15 fee$111 (−$33)
  4. 4 · Member, 18 sessions18 × $8 + $15 fee$159 (+$15)
  5. 5 · Break-even sessions($144 − $15) ÷ $8≈16 / mo
  6. 6 · Would travelers join?Saves 3 × 40 × $0.10 = $12, less than the $15 feeNo

Question 5: which levers first?

Interviewer

Here are six ways to raise station profit. Score each for ease and impact. Which three should Quillfield start with?

Your move

Score each lever with a one-line reason, then name your top three.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "I'd start with site selection, because the pilots show location decides whether a station pays back at all. Then time-of-day pricing, to fill quiet hours and charge more at peak. And idle fees, which are easy and free up chargers for paying customers. The membership is worth a small pilot, not a full launch."

    09

    The recommendation

    Interviewer

    The CEO is on the line. What do you recommend?

    Your move

    Lead with the answer to both decisions. Then reasons, risks, and next steps.

    Good answer

    "Keep the 45 cent price and launch the membership. A station breaks even at 25 sessions a day and pays back in about 6 years at 45 a day, and members who charge 18 times a month are worth 15 dollars more each month."

    Better answer

    "Two decisions. On location: only build where traffic supports at least 43 sessions a day. A station breaks even at 25, but it takes 43 to pay back in 7 years, and only 3 of the 8 pilots clear that bar, even though their average looks fine."

    "On pricing: keep 45 cents for everyone, and pilot the membership with commuters only. It pays only if members move from 12 to at least 16 sessions a month with us; if they don't, it's a 33 dollar monthly discount per member. The 15 dollar fee keeps travelers out, which is the right design."

    "Main risks: survey answers usually overstate behavior, and competitors may match the price. Next steps: traffic studies for all 40 sites, a 3-month membership pilot on two commuter corridors with a 16-session target, and idle fees plus time-of-day pricing everywhere."

    The good answer trusts the averages and the survey. The better answer finds the payback bar, sees the average trap, and turns the membership into a test with a clear pass mark.

    10

    Bonus drills: unit math

    Quick reps on the kind of math interviewer-led cases ask for.

    Quick reps

    1. Fixed costs are 90 thousand a year. Each session contributes 9 dollars. Break-even sessions a day, over 360 days?

    "About 28 a day. 90 thousand divided by 9 is 10 thousand a year, divided by 360."

    2. A station has 6 chargers. Each session takes 30 minutes. At 36 sessions a day, how many hours is each charger in use?

    "3 hours. 36 sessions is 18 charger-hours, split across 6 chargers. That's 12.5 percent of the day."

    3. The price rises from 45 to 50 cents and sessions don't change. By how much does contribution per session rise?

    "From 12 to 14 dollars, up about 17 percent, even though the price rose only 11 percent. Price changes hit margin harder than revenue."

    4. Why can an offer with a fee be smarter than a plain discount?

    "Because only frequent customers find it worth paying. Occasional customers keep paying full price, so the discount goes to the people whose extra visits you want."
